## Results and significance

- The considerations now concluded provide strong support for the hypotheses set out in . 2 concerning the interaction between matter and radiation through processes of absorption and emission, or through radiation incidence and radiation emission.
- The second Bohr rule (Equation 9) and Planck’s radiation formula followed naturally from the general quantum assumption for matter.
- However, what seems most important to me is the result concerning the momentum transferred to the molecule during incident and emitted radiation.
- If any of our assumptions regarding the latter were to be altered, this would result in a violation of equation (12); it seems hardly possible to remain consistent with this relationship, which is required by the theory of heat, other than on the basis of our assumptions.
- If the molecule is subjected to the action of several directed beams, only one of them is involved in any given elementary process of irradiation; this single beam then determines the direction of the momentum transferred to the molecule.
- These properties of the elementary processes, as required by equation (12), make the formulation of a truly quantum theory of radiation seem almost inevitable.
- One is easily tempted to do so, because the smallness of the momentum transferred by radiation means that, in reality, the latter almost always takes a back seat to other causes of motion.
- However, for theoretical consideration, these small effects must be regarded as entirely equivalent to the more obvious effects of energy transfer by radiation, since energy and momentum are most closely linked; a theory can therefore only be regarded as valid once it has been shown that the momentum transferred from radiation to matter, as described by that theory, leads to the kinds of motion required by the theory of heat.
