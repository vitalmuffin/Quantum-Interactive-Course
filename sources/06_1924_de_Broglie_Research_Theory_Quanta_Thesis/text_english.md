## HISTORICAL INTRODUCTION

### I. — Of the XVI$^{e}$ to the XX$^{e}$ century.

Modern science was born at the end of the sixteenth century$^{e}$ century following the intellectual revival due to the Renaissance. While positional astronomy became more precise from day to day, the sciences of balance and movement, statics and dynamics, were slowly constituted. It is known that it was Newton who first made Dynamics a homogeneous body of doctrine, and by his memorable law of universal gravitation opened up to the new science an enormous field of applications and verifications. During the eighteenth$^{e}$ and XIX$^{e}$ In the course of centuries a great number of geometers, astronomers, and physicists developed the principles of Newton, and mechanics attained such a degree of beauty and rational harmony that its character as a physical science was almost forgotten. In particular, it was possible to make the whole of this science flow from a single principle, the principle of least action enunciated first by Maupertuis, then in another way by Hamilton, and whose mathematical form is remarkably elegant and condensed.

## II. — The XX$^{e}$ century: Relativity and Quanta.

However, there were still some shadows in the picture. Lord Kelvin, in 1900, announced that two dark clouds were appearing threatening on the horizon of Physics. One of these clouds represented the difficulties raised by the famous experiment of Michelson and Morley, which seemed incompatible with the ideas received at the time. The second cloud represented the failure of the methods of Statistical Mechanics in the field of black radiation; the theorem of the equipartition of energy, a rigorous consequence of statistical mechanics, leads in fact to a well-defined distribution of energy between the various frequencies in the thermodynamic equilibrium radiation; but this law, the Rayleigh-Jeans law, is in gross contradiction with experiment and is even almost absurd because it predicts an infinite value for the total density of energy, which obviously makes no physical sense.

In the early years of the twentieth century$^{e}$ In the last century, Lord Kelvin's two clouds have, if I may say so, condensed, one into the theory of Relativity, the other into the theory of Quanta.

We will, on the contrary, briefly indicate the development of the theory of quanta. The notion of quantum was introduced into science in 1900 by Mr. Max Planck. At the time, this scientist was studying theoretically the question of black radiation and, as thermodynamic equilibrium should not depend on the nature of the emitters, he had imagined a very simple emitter called "Planck's resonator" consisting of an electron subjected to a quasi-elastic bond and thus possessing a vibration frequency independent of its energy. If we apply the classical laws of electromagnetism and statistical mechanics to the exchanges of energy between such resonators and radiation, we find Rayleigh's law, the undeniable inaccuracy of which we have pointed out above. To avoid this conclusion and to find results more in accordance with the experimental facts, Mr. Planck accepted a strange postulate: "The exchanges of energy between the resonators (or matter) and the radiation take place only in finite quantities equal to $h$ times the frequency, $h$ being a new universal constant in physics". Each frequency corresponds to a kind of energy atom, a *Quantum* of energy. The data of the observation furnished Mr. Planck with the necessary bases for the calculation of the constant $h$ and the value found then ($h = 6,545 \times 10^{-27}$) has hardly been modified by the innumerable subsequent determinations made by the most diverse methods. This is one of the finest examples of the power of theoretical physics.

## SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

In a brief history of the development of Physics since the seventeenth$^{e}$ century, and in particular of Dynamics and Optics, we have shown how the problem of quanta was in some way contained in the germ of the parallelism of the corpuscular and wave conceptions of radiation; then, we recalled with what intensity, increasing daily, the notion of quanta had imposed itself on the attention of the scholars of the twentieth$^{e}$ century.

In the first chapter, we accepted as a fundamental postulate the existence of a periodic phenomenon related to each isolated piece of energy and dependent on its own mass by the Planck-Einstein relation. The theory of Relativity then showed us the necessity of associating the constant velocity propagation of a certain "phase wave" with the uniform motion of any mobile and we were able to interpret this propagation by the consideration of Minkowski space-time.
