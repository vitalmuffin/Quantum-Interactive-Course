*On the Theory of the Law of Energy Distribution in the Normal Spectrum; by M. Planck.* (Presented at the meeting of 14 December 1900.)

(See p. 235 above.) M. H.! When, several weeks ago, I had the honour of drawing your attention to a new formula which seemed to me suitable for expressing the law of the distribution of radiant energy across all regions of the normal spectrum¹), my view of the formula’s usefulness, as I explained at the time, was based not only on the apparently good agreement between the few figures I was able to provide you with then and the measurement results available to date²), but chiefly on the simple structure of the formula and, in particular, on the fact that it yields a very simple logarithmic expression for the dependence of the entropy of an irradiated, monochromatically oscillating resonator on its oscillation energy; an expression which, in any case, seemed to offer greater promise of a general interpretation than any other formula proposed to date, apart from Wien’s, which, however, is not confirmed by the facts.

Entropy implies disorder, and I believed I could discern this disorder in the irregularity with which, even in a perfectly stationary radiation field, the oscillations of the resonator vary in amplitude and phase, provided one considers time intervals that are large compared with the period of one oscillation but small compared with the duration of a measurement. The second theorem forms the crux of the entire present theory; its proof can ultimately only be provided by experiment. It may also be regarded as a more precise formulation of the hypothesis of natural radiation which I have introduced, and which I have hitherto expressed only in the form that the energy of the radiation is distributed entirely ‘irregularly’ amongst the individual partial vibrations it contains.¹ I intend to present the considerations merely hinted at here in detail elsewhere in the near future, together with all the calculations, alongside a review of the theory’s development to date.

Finally, I should like to draw attention to an important consequence of the theory developed here, which at the same time allows for a further examination of its validity. Mr BOLTZMANN¹) has shown that the entropy of a monatomic gas at equilibrium is equal to ω R log ℜ_0, where ℜ_0 is the number of possible configurations (the ‘permutability’) under the most probable velocity distribution, R is the well-known gas constant (8.31 × 10⁷ for O = 16), and ω represents the ratio of the mass of a real molecule to the mass of a g-molecule, which is the same for all substances. If, however, radiating resonators are also present in the gas, then according to the theory developed here, the entropy of the entire system is proportional to the logarithm of the number of all possible complexions, velocities and radiation taken together. However, since, according to the electromagnetic theory of radiation, the velocities of the atoms are entirely independent of the distribution of the radiated energy, the total number of configurations is simply equal to the product of the numbers relating to the velocities and those relating to the radiation; consequently, the total entropy, where f’ denotes a proportionality factor, is:

$$f' \log (\mathfrak{P}_0 \mathfrak{R}_0) = f' \log \mathfrak{P}_0 + f' \log \mathfrak{R}_0.$$
The first term is the kinetic entropy, the second the radiative entropy. By comparison with the previous expressions, we obtain:

$$f = \omega R = k,$$
or

$$\omega = \frac{k}{R} = 1,62 \cdot 10^{-24},$$
i.e. a real molecule is 1.62 × 10⁻²⁴ times a g-molecule, or: a hydrogen atom weighs 1.64 × 10⁻²⁴ g, since H = 1.01, or: for every g-molecule of any substance, there are 1/ω = 6.175 × 10²³ real molecules. Mr O. E. MEYER²⁾ calculates this figure as 640 × 10²¹, which is therefore in close agreement.
