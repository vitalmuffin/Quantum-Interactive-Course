## Planck's Law and Light Quantum Hypothesis.

By Bose (Dacca University, India).

(Received July 2, 1924.)

The phase space of a light quantum with respect to a given volume is expressed in "cells" of the size $h^3$ divided. The number of possible distributions of the light quanta of a macroscopically defined radiation among these cells provides the entropy and thus all the thermodynamic properties of the radiation.

Planck's formula for the distribution of energy in the radiation of the black body forms the starting point for quantum theory, which has been developed over the last 20 years and has borne rich fruit in all fields of physics. Since its publication in 1901, many ways of deriving this law have been proposed. It is recognized that the fundamental premises of quantum theory are incompatible with the laws of classical electrodynamics. All previous derivatives make use of the relation

$$\varrho_v dv = \frac{8 \pi v^2 dv}{c^3} E,$$

i.e., the relation between the radiance and the average energy of an oscillator, and they make assumptions about the number of degrees of freedom of the aether as it enters into the above equation (first factor of the right side). However, this factor could only be derived from classical theory. This is the unsatisfactory point in all derivations, and it is not surprising that efforts are always made to give a derivation that is free from this logical error.

A remarkably elegant derivation has been given by Einstein. He recognized the logical deficiency of all previous derivatives and tried to deduce the formula independently of classical theory. Starting from very simple assumptions about the energy exchange between molecules and the radiation field, he finds the relation

$$\varrho_v = \frac{u_{mn}}{e^{\frac{\varepsilon_m - \varepsilon_n}{kT}} - 1}.$$

However, in order to bring this formula into harmony with Planck's, he must make use of Vienna's law of displacement and Bohr's principle of correspondence. Vienna's law is based on the classical

On the other hand, the light quantum hypothesis in connection with statistical mechanics (as it has been adapted by Planck to the needs of quantum theory) seems to me to be sufficient for the derivation of the law independently of classical theory. In the following, I will briefly outline the method.

The radiation is enclosed in the volume V and its total energy E is given. Let there be different types of quanta of the respective number N_s and energy h v_s (s = 0 to s = ∞). The total energy E is then

$$E = \sum_{s} N_{s} h \nu_{s} = V \int \varrho_{v} d\nu. \tag{1}$$

The solution of the problem then requires the determination of the N_s, which ρ_V. If we consider the probability for any_s, then the solution is determined by the condition that this probability should be a maximum if the constraint (1) is observed. We now want to look for this probability.

The quantum has a moment of the magnitude $$\frac{h \nu_{s}}{c}$$ in the direction of its locomotion. The instantaneous state of the quantum is characterized by its coordinates x, y, z and the corresponding moments p_x p_y p_z; these six quantities can be understood as point coordinates in a six-dimensional space, where we have the relation

$$p_{x}^{2} + p_{y}^{2} + p_{z}^{2} = \frac{h^{2} \nu^{2}}{c^{2}},$$

the said point is forced to remain on a cylinder surface determined by the frequency of the quantum. To the frequency range d v_s in this sense belongs to the phase space

$$\int dx \, dy \, dz \, dp_{x} \, dp_{y} \, dp_{z} = V \cdot 4\pi \left(\frac{h \nu}{c}\right)^{2} \frac{h d\nu}{c} = 4\pi \frac{h^{3} \nu^{2}}{c^{3}} V d\nu.$$

If we divide the entire phase volume into cells of the size h³, the frequency range d ν thus belongs to 4π V $$\frac{\nu^{2}}{c^{2}}$$ d ν cells. Nothing definite can be said as to the nature of this classification.

However, the total number of cells must be regarded as the number of possible arrangements of a quantum in the given volume. In order to take account of the fact of polarization, however, it seems necessary to multiply this figure by 2, so that we can calculate the number of polarizations to be $d\pmb{v}$ associated cells $8\pi V \frac{\pmb{v}^2 d\pmb{v}}{c^3}$ .

Now it is easy to calculate the thermodynamic probability of a (macroscopically defined) state. Let it be $N^s$ the number of $d\pmb{v}^s$ quanta. In how many ways can these be applied to the $d\pmb{v}^s$ cells? Be $p_0^s$ the number of vacant cells, $p_1^s$ the number of those that contain a quantum, $p_2^s$ the number of cells containing two quanta, etc. The number of possible distributions is then

$$\frac{A^s!}{p_0^s! p_1^s! \dots}, \text{ wobei } A^s = \frac{8\pi\pmb{v}^2}{c^3} d\pmb{v}^s,$$

and whereby

$$N^s = 0 \cdot p_0^s + 1 \cdot p_1^s + 2 p_2^s \dots$$

the number of $d\pmb{v}^s$ quanta.

The probability of the $p_r^s$ defined state is apparently

$$\prod_s \frac{A^s!}{p_0^s! p_1^s! \dots}.$$

In view of the fact that we have the $p_r^s$ as large numbers, we have

$$\lg W = \sum_s A^s \lg A^s - \sum_s \sum_r p_r^s \lg p_r^s,$$

whereby

$$A^s = \sum_r p_r^s.$$

This expression is supposed to be a maximum under the constraint

$$E = \sum_s N^s h \pmb{v}^s; \quad N^s = \sum_r r p_r^s.$$

Performing the variation provides the conditions

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_s \sum_r \delta p_r^s (1 + \lg p_r^s) &= 0, & \sum_s \delta N^s h \pmb{v}^s &= 0 \\ \sum_r \delta p_r^s &= 0 & \delta N^s &= \sum_r r \delta p_r^s. \end{aligned}$$

It follows from this

$$\sum_s \sum_r \delta p_r^s (1 + \lg p_r^s + \lambda^s) + \frac{1}{\beta} \sum_s h \pmb{v}^s \sum_r r \delta p_r^s = 0.$$

From this follows first of all

$$p_r^s = B^s e^{-\frac{r h v^s}{\beta}}.$$

But since

$$A^s = \sum_r B^s e^{-\frac{r h v^s}{\beta}} = B^s \left(1 - e^{-\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}\right)^{-1},$$

for example,

$$B_s = A^s \left(1 - e^{-\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}\right).$$

Furthermore, one has

$$\begin{aligned} N^s &= \sum_r r p_r^s = \sum_r r A^s \left(1 - e^{-\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}\right) e^{-\frac{r h v^s}{\beta}} \\ &= \frac{A^s e^{-\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}}{1 - e^{-\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}}. \end{aligned}$$

With regard to the value of $A^s$ is therefore

$$E = \sum_s \frac{8 \pi h v^{s^3} d v^s}{c^3} V \frac{e^{-\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}}{1 - e^{-\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}}.$$

Using the previous results, one can also find

$$S = k \left[ \frac{E}{\beta} - \sum_s A^s \lg \left(1 - e^{\frac{h v^s}{\beta}}\right) \right],$$

which, in view of the fact that $\frac{\partial S}{\partial E} = \frac{1}{T}$, it follows that $\beta = kT$. If you put this into the above equation for $E$ a, one obtains

$$E = \sum_s \frac{8 \pi h v^{s^3}}{c^3} V \frac{1}{e^{\frac{h v^s}{kT}} - 1} d v^s,$$

which equation Planck's formula is equivalent.
