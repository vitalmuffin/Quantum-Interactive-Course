### § 1. The physics of models.

In the second half of the last century, the great successes of the kinetic gas theory and the mechanical theory of heat had given rise to an ideal of an exact description of nature, which, as the culmination of centuries of research and the fulfilment of thousands of years of hope, is called the classical one. These are his features.

Of the natural objects whose observed behaviour one wants to grasp, based on the experimental data one possesses, but without resisting the intuitive imagination, one forms an idea that is precisely worked out in all its details. *a lot* more precisely than any experience can ever vouch for in view of its limited scope. The idea in its absolute determinateness resembles a mathematical structure or a geometrical figure, which consists of a number of *Intended items* can be calculated in its entirety; as, for example, on a triangle one side and the two angles adjacent to it, as determinants, help determine the third angle, the other two sides, the three heights, the radius of the inscribed circle, etc. The idea differs from a geometrical figure in its essence only in the important circumstance that it also appears in the *Time* as the fourth dimension is just as clearly defined as that in the three dimensions of space. This means that it is (which is self-evident) always a

structure that changes over time, the different *Conditions* can assume; and if a state is made known by the necessary number of determinants, then not only are all the other parts given at that moment (as explained above in the triangle), but all the parts, the exact state, are given at any definite later time; similar to how the nature of a triangle at the base determines its nature at the top. It is part of the inner law of the structure to change in a certain way, that is, when it is left to itself in a certain initial state, to go through a certain sequence of states continuously, each of which it reaches at a very definite time. That is its nature, that is the hypothesis which, as I said above, is posited on the basis of intuitive imagination.

Of course, one is not so simple-minded as to guess in this way how things really are in the world. To indicate that one does not think so, one likes to call the precise thinking aid that one has created for oneself a *Image* or a *Model*. With its ruthless clarity, which cannot be brought about without arbitrariness, it is only aimed at the fact that a very definite hypothesis can be tested in its consequences, without giving room to new arbitrariness during the lengthy calculations by which conclusions are derived. You have a bound route and actually only calculate what a clever Hans would read directly from the data! At least you know where the arbitrariness is and where you have to improve if the experience is not right: in the initial hypothesis, in the model. One must always be ready for this. If, in many different experiments, the natural object really behaves like the model, one rejoices and thinks that our picture is in essence in accordance with reality. If it is no longer true in a new experiment or in the refinement of the measuring technique, it is not said that one should be able to *not* . For basically, this is the way in which an ever better adaptation of the picture, that is, of our thoughts, to the facts can gradually succeed.

The classical method of the precise model has the main purpose of keeping the inevitable arbitrariness in the assumptions neatly isolated, I would almost like the body the germplasm, for the historical process of adaptation to progressive experience. Perhaps the

# § 2. The statistics of model variables in quantum mechanics.

At the pivotal point of today's quantum mechanics (Q.M.) is a doctrine that may yet undergo some reinterpretation, but, as I am firmly convinced, will not cease to form the pivotal point. It consists in the fact that models with determinants that clearly determine each other, like the classical ones, cannot do justice to nature.

One would think that for those who believe this, the classical models have played their role. But that's not the case. Rather, it is precisely they that are used, not only to express the negative of the new doctrine; but also the diminished mutual determination that remains afterwards is described as existing between the same variables of the same models that were previously used. Here's how.

A. The classical concept of the state is lost in that at most a well-selected half of a complete set of variables can be assigned certain numerical values; in the RUTHERFORD model, for example, the 6 right-angled coordinates or the velocity components (other groupings are also possible). The other half then remains completely indeterminate, while surplus pieces can show various degrees of indeterminacy. In general, in a complete movement (twelve pieces in the case of R.'s model), all of them will be known only vaguely. The best way to get information about the degree of uncertainty is, following classical mechanics, when selecting the variables for it

# § 4. Can one subordinate ideal totalities to theory?

The classic model plays a Proteus role in the Q.M. Each of his determinants can become an object of interest under certain circumstances and acquire a certain reality. But never all of them at the same time — sometimes it is this, sometimes it is that, and always at most half of a complete set of variables that would allow a clear picture of the current state. What about the others? Do they then have no reality, perhaps (s. v. v.) a blurred reality; or do they all always have one, and is it merely impossible, according to sentence A of § 2, to know them at the same time?

The second view is extremely obvious to those who know the significance of the statistical approach that emerged in the second half of the last century; especially when he remembers that on the eve of the new era, quantum theory was born out of it, out of a central problem of statistical thermodynamics (Max Planck's Theory of Thermal Radiation, December 1899). The essence of this school of thought consists precisely in the fact that one can practically never take into account all the determinants of the

§ 5. Are the variables really washed out?

The other alternative was to concede reality only to the sharp determinants — or, more generally, to allow each variable to be realized in such a way as corresponds exactly to the quantum mechanical statistics of these variables at that moment.

That it is not impossible to express the degree and nature of the washed-out nature of all variables in a perfectly clear picture is already evident from the fact that the Q.M. actually owns and uses such an instrument, the so-called wave function or ψ function, also called system vector. Much will be said about it below. That it is an abstract, incomprehensible mathematical structure is a concern that almost always arises with regard to new aids to thought and would not have much to say. In any case, it is a thing of thought that depicts the blurring of all variables at every moment just as clearly and exactly as the classical model depicts their sharp numerical values. Even its law of motion, the law of its temporal change as long as the system is left to itself, is not one iota inferior in clarity and definiteness to the equations of motion of the classical model. Thus, the ψ function could virtually take its place, as long as the washed-out is limited to atomic dimensions that are beyond direct control. In fact, quite vivid and convenient ideas have been derived from the function, for example, the "cloud of negative electricity" around the positive nucleus, and the like. Serious doubts arise, however, when one notices that indeterminacy takes hold of coarsely palpable and visible things, where the term "washed-out" then simply becomes false. The state of a radioactive nucleus is presumably washed out to such a degree and in such a way that neither the time of decay nor the direction in which the α particles that escape in the process leave the core are determined. § 6. The deliberate change of the epistemological standpoint.

In the fourth section we had seen that it is not possible to adopt the models outright and to ascribe certain values to the variables that are unknown or not exactly known, which we simply do not know. In § 5 we saw that the indeterminacy is not a real blur either, for there are at any rate cases where an easily executable observation provides the lack of knowledge. What is left now? The prevailing doctrine helps itself or us out of this very difficult dilemma by resorting to epistemology. We are told that no distinction is to be made between the real state of the natural object and what I know about it, or rather, perhaps, what I can know about it if I make an effort. *Really* — so they say — are really only perception, observation, measurement. If I have obtained through it at a given moment the best possible knowledge of the state of the physical object, which can be attained by natural law, then I may consider any further question about the "real state" as *irrelevant* insofar as I am convinced that no further observation can enlarge my knowledge of it — at least not without diminishing it in other respects by as much (namely, by changing the condition, see below).

This now throws some light on the genesis of the assertion, which I described at the end of § 2 as somewhat very far-reaching: that all model quantities are in principle measurable. One can hardly escape this article of faith if one finds oneself compelled to call upon the philosophical principle just mentioned, which, as the supreme patron of all empiricism, no intelligent person will refuse to respect, as a dictator in the troubles of physical methodology.

Reality resists mental reproduction by a model. One therefore leaves

naïve realism and is based directly on the indisputable thesis that *really* (for the physicist) is ultimately only the observation that is measurement. Then henceforth all our physical thinking has as its only basis and as the only object the results of measurements that can be carried out in principle, for our thinking is now supposed to be expressly directed to another kind of reality or to a model. *not* more. All numbers that appear in our physical calculations must be declared to be measures. However, since we are not just born and begin to rebuild our science, but have a very specific computing apparatus in use, from which we want to part less than ever since the great successes of the Q.M., we are forced to dictate from our desks which measurements are in principle possible, i.e. must be possible,  to sufficiently support our calculation scheme. This allows a sharp value for each model variable individually (even for a "half sentence"), so each one must be individually measurable with arbitrary accuracy. We must not be satisfied with less, because we have lost our naïve realist innocence. We have nothing but our calculation scheme to indicate where nature draws the ignorabimism line, i.e. what a *best possible* knowledge of the object. And if we could not do that, then our reality of measurement would depend very much on the diligence or laziness of the experimenter, how much effort he takes to inform himself. So we have to tell him how far he could get if he were only clever enough. Otherwise, it would be seriously feared that where we forbid further questions, there is still something worth knowing to ask.

§ 15. Natural principle or arithmetic artifice?

That the "sharp time" is an inconsistency within the Q.M. and that, moreover, independently of it, so to speak, the special position of time forms a serious obstacle to the adaptation of the Q.M. to the principle of relativity, I have pointed out again and again in recent years, unfortunately without being able to make the shadow of a useful counter-proposal. In surveying the whole situation of today, as I am trying to describe it here, a remark of a completely different kind comes to mind with regard to the "relativization" of the Q.M., which is so vehemently strived for, but has not yet really been achieved.

The strange theory of measurement, the apparent skipping of the ψ function, and finally the "antinomies of entanglement" all arise from the simple way in which the computational apparatus of quantum mechanics allows two separate systems to be mentally combined into one; for which he seems almost predestined. When two systems interact, as we have seen, it is not their ψ functions that interact, but they immediately cease to exist and a single one for the overall system takes their place. To mention this briefly, it initially consists simply of the product of the two individual functions; which, since one function depends on quite different variables than the other, is a function of all these variables, or "takes place in a region of much higher dimensional number" than the individual functions. As soon as the systems begin to interact with each other, the overall function ceases to be a product and also disintegrates when it changes again.
