There is a profound formal difference between the theoretical ideas which physicists have formed about gases and other ponderable bodies and Maxwell's theory of electromagnetic processes in so-called empty space. For while we consider the state of a body to be perfectly determined by the positions and velocities of a very large, but finite number of atoms and electrons, we make use of continuous spatial functions to determine the electromagnetic state of a space, so that a finite number of quantities is therefore not to be regarded as sufficient for the complete determination of the electromagnetic state of a space. According to Maxwell's theory, in all purely electromagnetic phenomena, including light, energy is to be understood as a continuous function of space, while the energy of a ponderable body is to be represented as a sum extended over the atoms and electrons according to the current view of physicists. The energy of a ponderable body cannot be divided into any number of arbitrarily small parts, while the energy of a ray of light emitted by a point-shaped light source is continuously distributed over an ever-increasing volume according to Maxwell's theory (or more generally according to any undulation theory).

The undulation theory of light, which operates with continuous spatial functions, has proven itself admirably for the representation of purely optical phenomena and will probably never be replaced by another theory.
