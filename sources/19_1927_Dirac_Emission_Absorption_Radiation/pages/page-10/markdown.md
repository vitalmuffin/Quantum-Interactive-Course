The transformation equations (8) must now be written in the quantum form

$$\left. \begin{array}{l} b_{r} = (N_{r} + 1)^{1/2} e^{i\Theta_{r}/h} = e^{-i\Theta_{r}/h} N_{r}^{1/2} \\ b_{r}^{*} = N_{r}^{1/2} e^{i\Theta_{r}/h} = e^{i\Theta_{r}/h} (N_{r} + 1)^{1/2}, \end{array} \right\} \tag{10}$$

in order that the $N_{r}, \Theta_{r}$ may also be canonical variables. These equations show that the $N$, can have only integral characteristic values not less than zero,$^{10}$ which provides us with a justification for the assumption that the variables are $q$-numbers in the way we have chosen. The numbers of systems in the different states are now ordinary quantum numbers.

The Hamiltonian (7) now becomes

$$F = \Sigma_{rs} b_{r}^{*} H_{rs} b_{s} = \Sigma_{rs} N_{r}^{1/2} e^{i\Theta_{r}/h} H_{rs} (N_{s} + 1)^{1/2} e^{-i\Theta_{s}/h} \tag{11}$$

$$= \Sigma_{rs} H_{rs} N_{r}^{1/2} (N_{s} + 1 - \delta_{rs})^{1/2} e^{i(\Theta_{r} - \Theta_{s})/h}$$

in which the $H_{rs}$ are still $c$-numbers. We may write this $F$ in the form corresponding to (9)

$$F = \Sigma_{r} W_{r} N_{r} + \Sigma_{rs} v_{rs} N_{r}^{1/2} (N_{s} + 1 - \delta_{rs})^{1/2} e^{i(\Theta_{r} - \Theta_{s})/h} \tag{11'}$$

in which it is again composed of a proper energy term $\Sigma_{r} W_{r} N_{r}$ and an interaction energy term.

The wave equation written in terms of the variables $N_{r}$ is$^{11}$

$$i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi(n'_{1}, N'_{2}, N'_{3} \dots) = F \psi(N'_{1}, N'_{2}, N'_{3} \dots), \tag{12}$$

where $F$ is an operator, each $\Theta_{r}$ occurring in $F$ being interpreted to mean $i\hbar \partial/\partial N'_{r}$. If we apply the operator $e^{\pm i\Theta_{r}/h}$ to any function $f(N'_{1}, N'_{2}, N'_{3}, \dots)$ of the variables $N'_{1}, N'_{2}, \dots$ the result is

$$\begin{array}{rl} e^{\pm i\Theta_{r}/h} f(N'_{1}, N'_{2}, \dots N'_{r}, \dots) & = e^{\pm \partial/\partial N'_{r}} f(N'_{1}, N'_{2}, \dots N'_{r} \dots) \\ & = f(N'_{1}, N'_{2}, \dots N'_{r} \mp 1, \dots). \end{array}$$

$^{10}$See § 8 of the author's paper "Roy. Soc. Proc.," A, vol. III, p. 281 (1926). What are there called the $c$-number values that a $q$-number can take are here given the more precise name of the characteristic values of that $q$-number.

$^{11}$We are supposing for definiteness that the label $r$ of the stationary states takes the values 1, 2, 3...
